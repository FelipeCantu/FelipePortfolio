Most advice for new freelance developers stops at "build a portfolio and be good at what you do." That part matters, but it isn't what sinks people. What sinks them is the work around the code: a vague quote that turns into three months of unpaid extras, a client who goes quiet when the final invoice arrives, or a tax bill in April nobody saved for.

This post covers that business side: the process I use on client work, from first inquiry to the monthly plan after launch, with the reasoning so you can adapt it.

## Find your first clients without depending on a marketplace

Upwork and similar marketplaces are a reasonable way to get your first few paid projects. Clients there already have a budget and a problem, and a finished contract with a review gives you something to show the next client.

Treat them as a start, not a plan. You compete on price against a global pool, you pay to bid (Upwork sells "Connects" for proposals), and the platform takes a service fee on what you earn. Upwork's current freelancer fee varies from 0% to 15% per contract, and you see the exact number before you submit. More importantly, the client relationship lives on someone else's platform, under someone else's rules.

The clients who stick around usually come from closer to home:

- People you already know who run a business, a nonprofit or a side project.
- Referrals from past clients, which you get by asking directly once a project goes well.
- Other developers and designers who have more work than they can take, or who need a specialist.
- Your own site, with case studies that explain the problem, what you built and why.

A case study of a nonprofit site with articles, a shop and Stripe donations tells a potential client more than a list of frameworks. Write each one as "here's what they needed, here's what I built," and give the reader one obvious next step.

## Go from inquiry to written proposal

### Start with an intake form and a free call

On my site, that next step is a **Get Started** form. It collects the basics before we ever talk: what the person wants built, the rough timeline and a budget range.

A form filters out people who aren't ready, and I show up to the first conversation already knowing the shape of the project. The budget question feels awkward. Ask it anyway; a range is enough, and it saves both sides an hour when the numbers are far apart.

Then comes a free call. Keep it short. The goal isn't to sell. It's to understand the business problem behind the feature list. "We need a new website" often means "we're losing leads because nobody can book online." That second sentence is what your proposal should solve.

By the end of the call you should know who makes the decision, who will send you content, what "done" looks like to them, and whether you actually want the project.

### Scope it in writing and offer options

After the call, I send a written proposal. A good one is short and specific:

- **The problem**, in the client's own words from the call.
- **What's included**: pages, features, integrations, number of revision rounds.
- **What's not included**, stated plainly. Copywriting, photography, ongoing SEO and third-party fees are common ones.
- **Milestones** with rough dates, each with a clear deliverable.
- **Price and payment schedule.**
- **What you need from them** and by when (content, logins, brand assets).

The "not included" list does more to prevent arguments than anything else in the document. If it isn't written down as in scope, it's a conversation for later, not an assumption.

I also like to offer options, usually two or three: a core build, the same build plus the feature they mentioned as a nice-to-have, and sometimes a phased version that launches smaller and adds the rest later. Options change the client's question from "should I hire this person?" to "which version fits?" They also give budget-conscious clients a way to say yes without you cutting your rate.

## Price by the project, quote in ranges, take a deposit

### Project-based or hourly

For well-defined builds, I prefer project-based pricing. The client knows the cost up front, and you're rewarded for working efficiently instead of penalized for it. The risk is on you to scope well, which is why the proposal step matters so much.

Hourly makes sense when the scope genuinely can't be known yet: debugging someone else's codebase, open-ended consulting, or small ongoing tasks. If you bill hourly, track time honestly and send a summary with each invoice so nothing looks padded.

### Quote ranges early, fixed numbers late

On the call, if a client asks "roughly how much?", give a range, not a number. "Sites like this usually fall between X and Y depending on how the shop and booking pieces shake out" sets expectations without locking you into a figure before you've scoped anything. The written proposal is where the range becomes a fixed price per option.

To find your numbers, estimate the hours, multiply by a rate that covers taxes, software and slow months, then add a buffer for unknowns. Don't forget the unpaid hours (calls, proposals, invoicing); your billable rate has to pay for those too.

### Why a deposit is non-negotiable

No work starts until the agreement is signed and the deposit is paid. Splitting the price across milestones, with a deposit up front and the rest due as each milestone is delivered, is a common structure, and it works because both sides have something at stake.

A deposit confirms the client is serious, pays for your time if they disappear halfway, and reserves a slot in your calendar.

> No signed agreement and no deposit means no work. Not "just a quick start on the design while the paperwork goes through." That habit is how most unpaid projects begin.

## Put it in a contract: what every agreement needs

This is not legal advice. Contract law varies by state and country, so have a lawyer review your template once, then reuse it. That review costs far less than a single dispute.

With that said, these are the terms I'd expect in any freelance development agreement:

- **Scope**, by referencing the accepted proposal and option.
- **Payment terms**: amounts, due dates, accepted methods and what happens when a payment is late.
- **Revisions**: how many rounds are included and how extra rounds are billed.
- **Change requests**: how new work gets requested, priced and approved.
- **Client responsibilities**: content, feedback windows and access, plus what happens to the timeline if they're late.
- **Ownership**: when the client owns the code and design (commonly on final payment) and what you can show in your portfolio.
- **Termination**: how either side can end the project and what's owed for work done.
- **Warranty and support**: how long you'll fix bugs for free after launch, and where support becomes paid work.

Signing doesn't need to be a printed PDF and a scanner. In my client portal, the client reviews the proposal, signs the agreement electronically and pays the deposit in one place, so there's never a question about which version they agreed to.

## Keep the project on track: change requests and updates

### Price scope creep as change requests

Scope creep rarely arrives as a big demand. It's "can we also add a login?" in the middle of a feedback email. Each request sounds small, and saying yes to all of them quietly turns a fixed-price project into a loss.

The fix isn't saying no. It's routing every new idea through a change request:

1. Acknowledge the idea and say you'll price it separately.
2. Write a short change request: what it is, the cost, and how it moves the timeline.
3. Wait for written approval (and payment, if it's large) before starting.
4. Add it to the project as its own item so the client can see it next to the original scope.

Explain this on the first call and in the contract, and most clients are fine with it. What frustrates them is a surprise on the final invoice, not a clear price up front.

### Put communication on a schedule

Silence makes clients nervous, and nervous clients send a lot of "just checking in" emails. A predictable cadence heads that off.

I'd set three expectations at kickoff: how often you'll send updates (a short weekly summary works well), how quickly you'll reply to messages on business days, and where things live. For my projects, that's the client portal. Clients can see each milestone and its status, approve files, look at site previews and open support requests without digging through email threads.

You don't need to build a portal to get the same effect. A shared project board plus a weekly email that answers "what got done, what's next, what I need from you" covers most of it.

## Get paid on time and keep enough for taxes

### Make paying easy and follow up on a schedule

Make paying easy. Send invoices with a clear due date, line items that match the milestones, and a way to pay online. My invoices are paid through Stripe in the portal, so the client clicks a link and is done. Every extra step between "invoice received" and "paid" is a reason for it to sit.

Even with a good process, some invoices go late. Usually it's an approval stuck with someone else, not bad intent, so start friendly and get firmer on a schedule:

- **A few days before the due date**: a short reminder with the link.
- **On the due date**: "Just a heads up that this is due today."
- **A week late**: a direct note asking if anything is holding it up and when you can expect payment.
- **Two weeks or more**: pause new work, as your contract allows, and refer to the late-payment terms.

Keep every message brief and factual. Pausing work politely beats resenting a client for months.

### Basic US tax and money hygiene

None of this is tax advice; rules change and depend on your situation, so confirm the details with an accountant. That said, a few basics apply to most US freelancers:

- **Open a separate business bank account.** Every client payment goes in, every business expense comes out. It makes bookkeeping and tax time far simpler.
- **Set aside a percentage of every payment** in a separate savings account the day it arrives. Many freelancers start with 25 to 30 percent and adjust once an accountant has looked at their numbers.
- **Remember self-employment tax.** On top of income tax, you pay both halves of Social Security and Medicare on your net self-employment earnings, which surprises a lot of people who are used to a paycheck.
- **Pay quarterly estimated taxes.** According to the [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/faqs/estimated-tax), you generally need to make estimated payments if you expect to owe $1,000 or more for the year. The due dates are April 15, June 15, September 15 and January 15, moved to the next business day when they fall on a weekend or holiday.
- **Keep records of all income**, not just what shows up on a 1099. Some clients won't send one, and the income is still taxable.

## Build recurring revenue with care plans

Project work is lumpy: booked solid one month, writing proposals the next. The easiest way for a web developer to smooth that out is an optional monthly care plan after launch.

A care plan might cover hosting management, dependency and security updates, uptime monitoring, backups, small content edits and a set amount of support time each month. For the client, it means someone who knows the codebase is responsible for keeping it healthy. For you, it's predictable income and a natural path to the next project, because you're the first person they call when they want a new feature.

Offer it in the proposal as an option rather than a surprise at launch, and define the limits clearly: what's included each month, what counts as new work, and how either side can cancel.

## What to set up this week

You don't need all of this before your first client, but each piece removes a category of problems. Starting from scratch, I'd go in this order:

1. An intake form on your site and a standard free-call agenda.
2. A proposal template with a "not included" section and two or three options.
3. A contract template, reviewed once by a lawyer, with deposit and change-request terms.
4. Online invoicing and a reminder schedule for late payments.
5. A separate bank account and a tax savings percentage.
6. A care plan you can offer at the end of every project.

The developers who last as freelancers aren't always the best coders. They're the ones whose clients always know what they're paying for, what's happening next and how to say yes.
