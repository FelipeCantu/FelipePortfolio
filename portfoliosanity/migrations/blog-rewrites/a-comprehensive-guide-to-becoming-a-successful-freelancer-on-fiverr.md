You can build a solid Next.js app, but nobody is sending you work yet. Fiverr looks like a shortcut: clients already there, payments handled. It can be, if you treat it as a storefront with strict house rules.

This guide covers how to shape a development gig so it doesn't turn into unpaid work, what Fiverr takes and when you get paid, how levels are earned, which rules get accounts suspended, and when to build your own channel instead.

## Decide whether your work fits a gig at all

Fiverr is built around productized services: a fixed scope, a fixed price, a delivery date. That fits some development work very well and other work badly.

Good fits are tasks you can describe completely before you start:

- Converting a Figma design into a responsive Next.js or React page
- Fixing a specific bug, build error or failed deployment
- Setting up Stripe Checkout on an existing site
- Speed and Core Web Vitals fixes on a known stack
- Connecting a headless CMS like Sanity to an existing front end

Poor fits are open-ended builds: "an app like Airbnb," a marketplace with user accounts and payments, anything where the requirements are still being discovered. Those belong in custom offers with milestones, not your first gig.

My advice is to pick one stack and one outcome. "I'll fix your Next.js build or deployment errors on Vercel" is easier to find, easier to price and easier to deliver well than "Full-stack developer, any language." Before you commit, search the exact phrase a client would type and read the top gigs. Note their prices, what each tier includes, and what reviews complain about. Those gaps (slow replies, missed details, no handoff notes) are what you will compete on.

## Build packages that hold up against scope creep

Every gig can have up to three packages, usually labeled Basic, Standard and Premium, plus optional extras. For development work the packages are your contract, so write them like one.

Define each tier by a measurable deliverable, not by effort. "Up to 1 page, responsive, built from your Figma file" is enforceable. "Basic website" is not. For each package, state:

- What gets built, in countable units (pages, components, endpoints, integrations)
- What the client must provide (design files, copy, API keys, repository access)
- What is excluded (content writing, hosting costs, third-party plugin fees)
- Delivery time and the number of revisions

Revisions deserve care. A revision means changing what was agreed, not adding new features. Say that in your gig description and FAQ, and keep revision counts low on cheaper tiers. Unlimited revisions on a development gig is how a two-hour job becomes a two-week one.

Use the gig's requirements questions to collect what you need before the clock starts: the repository or a zip of the code, the framework version, the hosting provider, a link to the design, and a short description of "done." If the client can't answer those, they probably need a custom offer instead of a package.

Extras are where reasonable upsells live: faster delivery, an additional page, a deployment to the client's own hosting, a short written handoff document, or a set period of post-delivery fixes. Price them so each extra is worth your time on its own.

### Use milestones for bigger builds

For larger projects, Fiverr supports milestones, which split one order into stages that are delivered and paid separately. According to Fiverr's help center, milestones are available in certain subcategories (website design is one), the gig's basic package must be at least $100, each milestone must be at least $50, and a gig can have two to five milestones. Custom offers can include up to six.

The client pays for the first milestone up front and each following one after approving the previous delivery. Revisions are set per milestone. That maps onto how you'd work anyway (design approval, core build, integrations, launch), and a disagreement in week three doesn't put the whole project at risk.

## Set up a profile and gig page that answer a client's questions

Clients scanning search results decide quickly. Your profile and gig page should answer three things fast: can this person do my exact task, have they done it before, and will they communicate.

Use a clear, recent photo of your face. In your bio, lead with what you build and for whom, then name the stack. A line like "I build fast Next.js and Tailwind sites for small businesses and fix the deployment problems that block launches" tells a client more than a paragraph about passion.

Write the gig title in the client's words, starting with the outcome: "I will fix your Next.js build or Vercel deployment errors." Put the terms people actually search for in the title, tags and description naturally. Don't stuff keywords.

The description should show your process: what you need from the client, how you report progress, and what they receive at the end. For developers, say you deliver clean commits or a pull request, a short README on how to run and deploy it, and a list of anything you couldn't fix and why.

Portfolio pieces beat claims. Use screenshots or short screen recordings of real work, with a sentence on the problem and what you did. An intro video is optional; if you make one, a calm walkthrough of a past project on screen beats a staged talking-head clip.

## Know the fees, payouts and seller levels before you price anything

Fiverr keeps 20% of each order. Its help center describes it as freelancers receiving 80% of the client's cleared payment. Price with that in mind: a $250 package puts $200 in your balance before payout fees or taxes.

After an order is marked complete, earnings are held before you can withdraw them. The standard hold is 14 days. Top Rated, Fiverr Pro and Seller Plus freelancers get a shorter period, which Fiverr lists as 7 days in its milestones documentation. An order completes when the client accepts the delivery or doesn't respond within three days. So the real gap between finishing work and having cash is closer to three weeks for a new seller. Plan your first months with that delay in mind.

Fiverr's level system is evaluated daily against six metrics. Per the help center:

- **Level 1:** success score 5+, rating 4.4+, 80% response rate, 5 orders, 3 unique clients, $400 earned
- **Level 2:** success score 7+, rating 4.6+, 90% response rate, 20 orders, 10 unique clients, $2,000 earned
- **Top Rated:** success score 9+, rating 4.7+, 90% response rate, 40 orders, 20 unique clients, $10,000 earned, plus a manual review of your overall performance

Response rate counts new messages answered within 24 hours over the last 90 days. Reply every day, not every minute. The success score is based on your order history and client relationships compared with similar freelancers, so cancellations and unhappy orders weigh on it. Drop below a level's criteria and you get a 30-day grace period before you move down.

Fiverr Pro is separate from levels. It's an application-based, vetted tier with no membership fee. Fiverr looks at your portfolio (including work done outside Fiverr), your workflow and tools, and your track record on communication and delivery. If you already have strong client work, it's worth applying once your category is open, since Pro is aimed at business clients with bigger budgets.

## Stop waiting for Buyer Requests

Older guides tell you to spend an hour a day answering Buyer Requests. That feature is gone. Fiverr replaced it with Briefs, where a client's project was sent to a small group of matched freelancers, and Fiverr's help center now says Briefs itself has been fully replaced by Mira, an AI headhunter.

With Mira, a client describes the project, Mira drafts a job description, and then it messages freelancers it considers a fit, asking them a few questions before presenting a shortlist to the client. Matching leans on your past Fiverr projects and experience level. For budgets under $250, Mira doesn't contact freelancers at all and just shows the client a filtered list.

Answer Mira's first message within 24 hours, because it counts toward your response rate. Your answers also shape how you're presented to the client, so treat them like a short proposal: name the specific part of the project you'd handle first and the one question you'd need answered.

Because the matching rewards history, new sellers get most of their early work from search. That's another reason to keep your first gig narrow and well described.

## Deliver development work without breaking the rules

This is the section that keeps your account alive. Fiverr's position is clear: payments must stay on Fiverr, and you can't move a client to WhatsApp, Discord, email or your own site to do business off the platform. Fiverr's off-platform guidance says third-party communication tools are only allowed after an order is placed, and that you should summarize those conversations back in the Fiverr inbox. Violations start with warnings and can end in suspension, and anyone transacting off-platform loses Fiverr's protection.

> Never ask a Fiverr client to pay you outside Fiverr, and never send them your email, phone number or website to "continue directly." It is the fastest way to lose the account and every review on it.

Web projects do need some outside access, and Fiverr acknowledges that building a website is a legitimate case. Handle it carefully:

- Ask to be invited as a collaborator on GitHub, Vercel or the CMS instead of asking for passwords. Fiverr's guidance says not to share passwords at all, and collaborator access is safer for both of you anyway.
- Don't use or accept remote-control tools like TeamViewer or AnyDesk; Fiverr specifically warns against them.
- Keep scope, approvals and revision requests in the Fiverr order, even if you discussed them on a call.
- Deliver proof inside the order: a link to the pull request, a staging URL, screenshots or a short screen recording of the working feature.

Reviews follow from delivery, not from asking. Deliver a little before the deadline, include a short summary of what changed and how to verify it, and flag anything you noticed but didn't touch. Don't offer discounts or extras in exchange for a review, and don't pressure clients to change one. When a review is unfair, respond once, briefly and professionally.

## Run it like a business from the first order

Fiverr makes it easy to overbook yourself because orders arrive with deadlines already attached. Set delivery times you can hit with a buffer, and use the availability setting when you're full rather than accepting work you'll deliver late. A slow month costs you income; late deliveries and cancellations also cost you the success score future orders depend on.

Keep the money separate. Withdraw to a dedicated business account, track each order's gross price, Fiverr's cut and your net, and set aside part of every payout for taxes, because nobody is withholding them for you. Talk to an accountant about your situation; the rules depend on where you live and how you're set up.

## Know when to build your own channel

Fiverr is good at getting you your first paying clients and reviews. It's weaker at the work that makes freelancing sustainable: larger projects, long-term retainers and clients who come to you directly. You are also paying 20% on every order, forever, and your visibility depends on an algorithm you don't control.

The honest path is to build a second channel alongside Fiverr, not to pull Fiverr clients off it. Clients who found you on Fiverr stay on Fiverr; if they want ongoing work, use Fiverr's own custom offers or subscription orders. New clients who find you through your site, referrals, LinkedIn or GitHub can work with you directly.

That second channel is a personal site that shows your work and a clear way to start a project. On my own site I run a client portal that handles proposals, agreements with e-signature, Stripe invoices, file approvals, site previews and support requests. That's the structure Fiverr gives you for free, and it's worth replicating before you rely on direct clients. Without it, you chase invoices over email. With it, a retainer client gets the same structure as on a marketplace, and you keep the full fee.

Pick one narrow, fixed-scope development task you're already good at. Search for it on Fiverr and read the top gigs and their reviews. Write three packages with countable deliverables, clear exclusions and limited revisions, and set up requirements questions that collect repo access and a definition of done. Price with the 20% cut and the 14-day hold in mind, reply to every message within a day, and keep every payment and decision inside the order.

Then start your own site in parallel, so the reviews you earn on Fiverr eventually point to a business you own.
